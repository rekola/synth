# The extended diamond: gamut, scales and chords

## Overview

Otonal and utonal are the application's two default scales. Both are drawn
from one gamut of seventeen just ratios, the extended diamond, and each is
the other turned upside down (r → 2/r). They are given here on C and can be
built on any tonic. Each has four sizes:

| Size | Otonal | Utonal | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | C D♯ E F G A A♯↑ | C E𝄫 E♭ F G A♭ B𝄫 | one harmonic (undertone) series, two seventh chords |
| 8 | C D♯ E F F♯↑ G A A♯↑ | C E𝄫 E♭ F G♭↓ G A♭ B𝄫 | the 7 with the tritone |
| 9 | C D♭↓ D♯ E F G A A♯↑ B | C D♭↓ E𝄫 E♭ F G A♭ B𝄫 B | the 7 with the semitones either side of the tonic |
| 10 | C D♭↓ D♯ E F F♯↑ G A A♯↑ B | C D♭↓ E𝄫 E♭ F G♭↓ G A♭ B𝄫 B | the full scale, four seventh chords |

Three things set them apart from the major and minor scales they resemble:

1. **Chords use only the gamut's intervals.** Every interval between two
   notes of a chord is itself a ratio of the gamut, so a chord never contains
   an interval the scales do not have (9/7, for one).
2. **Every note has a seventh chord.** In every scale each degree belongs to
   a harmonic seventh, 4:5:6:7, or its mirror, made of the scale's own
   notes.
3. **Nothing drifts.** Every note has one fixed ratio to the tonic. There is
   no D, so the diatonic scale's wolf fifth (D–A = 40/27) and the comma pumps
   of 5-limit just intonation (I–vi–ii–V–I losing 81/80 per cycle) cannot
   occur. Within a scale no two notes are closer than 21/20 (84 cents); the
   35-cent pairs D♯/E𝄫 and F♯↑/G♭↓ are split between the two scales.

**Notation.** Notes are spelled as in meantone: 7/4 is the augmented sixth
A♯, 7/6 the augmented second D♯, 8/7 the diminished third E𝄫, 12/7 the
diminished seventh B𝄫, 7/5 the augmented fourth F♯ and 10/7 the diminished
fifth G♭. An arrow after a note raises or lowers it by one step of 53-EDO (a
syntonic comma); the other tunings ignore it. So 7/4 is A♯↑, since 53-EDO's
plain A♯ is 26/15, and likewise F♯↑, G♭↓ and D♭↓. D♭↓ and B each name two
ratios, one per scale. Degrees are named by letter, as in the diatonic
scale, with the note's accidental attached, so that IV is always the fourth
and V the fifth. Chords are written lowest note first; a proportion in
fractions is the same chord as the whole numbers beside it.

The Scale column gives the scale a ratio belongs to, with the smallest size
that has it when that is not 7. The seventeen ratios lie on fifteen keys: the
two ratios of D♭↓ share a key, as do the two of B, 8 cents apart (225/224),
and the scale decides which one the key carries. The gamut mirrors itself
around the tonic: in 53-EDO step n ↔ 53 − n.

**Construction.** The gamut is built from the tonic by dividing intervals at
their arithmetic and harmonic means.

1. Divide the octave C–C′: G (arithmetic mean, 3/2) and F (harmonic mean,
   4/3). Both belong to both scales.
2. Divide C–F, C–G, F–C′ and G–C′: the pairs (7/6, 8/7), (5/4, 6/5),
   (5/3, 8/5) and (7/4, 12/7). The larger of each pair, the arithmetic mean,
   is otonal; the smaller, the harmonic mean, utonal. These eleven notes are
   the two 7-note scales.
3. Divide the remaining fifths inside each scale and keep the means that are
   new and are ratios between two of 1, 3, 5 and 7. There is one for each:
   7/5 divides D♯–A♯↑ and joins otonal, 10/7 divides E𝄫–B𝄫 and joins
   utonal. The thirteen notes so far are the 7-limit tonality diamond, which
   is all such ratios.
4. Divide the intervals from the tonic to its four nearest neighbours and
   keep the means with no prime above 7: C–E𝄫 gives the pair (15/14, 16/15)
   and A♯↑–C′ the pair (15/8, 28/15), the larger again otonal. C–D♯ and
   B𝄫–C′ give ratios of 13 and are left out.

## The scales

**7 notes.** Otonal is harmonics 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20 and 21 of the F a
fifth below the tonic, and is the two harmonic seventh chords C E G A♯↑ and
F A C D♯ put together. Utonal is the same run of undertones of the G a fifth
above the tonic, the chords E𝄫 F A♭ C and B𝄫 C E♭ G.

**10 notes.** The full scale makes two additions to the 7, each bringing a
seventh chord, so that every note still has one.

- *The tritone.* F♯↑ in otonal, G♭↓ in utonal, with a chord made of notes
  the scale already has: C D♯ F♯↑ A♯↑ in otonal, E𝄫 G♭↓ B𝄫 C in utonal. It
  is a chord of the other kind (a mirror chord in otonal, a harmonic seventh
  in utonal) and holds a new triad, the minor D♯ F♯↑ A♯↑ (in utonal the
  major E𝄫 G♭↓ B𝄫). With the tritone the scale's pentatonic becomes a blues
  scale.
- *The semitones.* D♭↓ and B, either side of the tonic, with a chord of
  their own: D♭↓ E G B (15/14 5/4 3/2 15/8, 1/7:1/6:1/5:1/4) in otonal,
  D♭↓ F A♭ B (16/15 4/3 8/5 28/15, 4:5:6:7) in utonal. It is the one seventh
  chord that does not contain the tonic, so the harmony can move away from
  it. It also gives E a pure fifth and the minor triad E G B (in utonal
  D♭↓ F A♭, major).

**8 and 9 notes.** Each takes one of the two additions: the 8-note scale
the tritone, the 9-note scale the semitones. They have nothing the 10-note
scale lacks. What sets them apart is what they leave out: in the 8-note
scale every seventh chord still contains the tonic, as in the 7, which
suits music over a drone; the 9-note scale has harmony that leaves the
tonic but not the cluster of small steps around the tritone. They are
there for the Launchpad's step view, which steps through the scale's notes.

**Pentatonic subscales.** Each scale contains a pentatonic with steps of
7/6, 8/7 and 9/8 and no semitone, which may be useful for melody. Otonal has
C D♯ F G A♯↑ (1/1 7/6 4/3 3/2 7/4), the septimal minor pentatonic, with the
shape of C E♭ F G B♭. Utonal has its mirror, C E𝄫 F G B𝄫 (1/1 8/7 4/3 3/2
12/7), with the shape of C D F G A; begun on F it has the shape of the
major pentatonic. They are not scales of their own: not every note of a
pentatonic has a chord within it, and the full scale offers far more
harmony. The ordinary pentatonic (C D E G A) needs a chain of four fifths
and does not occur.

## Chords

A chord is any set of notes in which every interval is a ratio of the gamut.
Ten of the gamut's four-note chords are seventh chords, whose intervals are
all ratios of the diamond: the harmonic seventh 4:5:6:7 on C, F, A♭, E𝄫 and
D♭↓ (16/15), and its mirror 1/7:1/6:1/5:1/4 below C′, G, E, A♯↑ and B
(15/8).

A degree's chord is a seventh chord it belongs to, read upward from the
degree. In practice the fourth note is often dropped, which also keeps two
degrees of one seventh chord from sounding alike.

The tables are for the 10-note scales. A smaller scale has the rows of its
own notes and the chords made of them: a chord with F♯↑ (in utonal G♭↓)
needs the tritone, one with D♭↓ or B the semitones. The last column is the
major, minor or narrow triad the degree roots, where it has one.

Major and minor divide the fifth at its arithmetic and harmonic means;
narrow major and narrow minor do the same for the fourth. The subminor and
supermajor triads (6:7:9, 14:18:21) are not chords here, since 9/7 is not a
ratio of the gamut.

**Other chords.** Beyond the seventh chords, the rule admits chords that
contain a semitone, 15/14 or 16/15. The otonal scales have the major
seventh chord F A C E (8:10:12:15) and, with B, C E G B as well; utonal has
their mirrors. Two chords of the 7-note otonal scale sound D♯ (7/6) against E (5/4), a
15/14 apart:

- C D♯ E A♯↑ (12:14:15:21) is the harmonic seventh without its fifth, with
  D♯ added: a 7♯9 chord with no fifth. The 7♯9 is nicknamed the Hendrix
  chord, after the E7♯9 of Jimi Hendrix's "Purple Haze", though jazz players
  used it earlier. Its sound is the major third held against the blue third,
  and convention spells that sharp ninth D♯, as this notation spells 7/6. So
  the chord appears here with its blue note at the pure septimal third that
  blues players bend towards.
- C D♯ E A (12:14:15:20) is the minor triad A C E with D♯, a 7/5 above the
  A: A minor with its diminished fifth sounding as well.

## Tuning

Played live, recorded or typed, and after `apply-just-intonation-region`, each
note of the gamut sounds at its exact ratio to the tonic
([user-interface.md](user-interface.md#just-intonation)), so a note borrowed
from the other scale is tuned as well. In 31 and 53-EDO every gamut note has
a key of its own, at the steps in the table above, except that D♭↓ and B
each carry the ratio of the song's scale: 15/14 and 15/8 in otonal, 16/15
and 28/15 in utonal. Without correction both tunings come within 6.2 cents
of every ratio. 12 and 19-EDO only approximate the septimal ratios, and
there more gamut notes share a key; the song's scale decides which one the
key carries.

## Technical details

**The diamond.** The 7-limit tonality diamond was first set out by Max Meyer
(1929); Harry Partch's diamond is its 11-limit form. Its thirteen notes are
exactly the notes of the eight seventh chords that contain the tonic, and
its ratios are exactly the intervals inside one of them.

**The extension.** The four added ratios lie outside the diamond, which has
no 15. They complete the pairs of the construction, an otonal and a utonal
ratio for every division, and bring the two seventh chords that do not
contain the tonic. Partch's 43-note scale has two of the four, 16/15 and
15/8.

**Chord counts.** The gamut has 54 four-note chords and ten five-note ones,
each of the ten a four-note chord without the tonic and the tonic itself;
there is no six-note chord. The 7-note scales hold seven four-note chords
each, the 10-note scales seventeen.

**Series.** Every note of the 7-note otonal scale is a harmonic of the F a
fifth below the tonic, and the tonic is harmonic 12, identity 3, not the
fundamental. B (15/8) is harmonic 45 of that F; the tritone and D♭↓ are not
in the series. In Partch's terms the 7-note scale is the Otonalities on 1/1
and 4/3 put together; his own Otonalities and Utonalities are chords.

**Sources.** The means are Zarlino's (1558), told for the present day in Kyle
Gann's *The Arithmetic of Listening* (2019); the diamond is Meyer's (*The
Musician's Arithmetic*, 1929) and otonality and utonality are Partch's
(*Genesis of a Music*, 1949); the mirror is the harmonic dualism of
Hauptmann, von Oettingen and Riemann. The scales, the extension of the
diamond, the chord rule and the narrow triads with their names are the
author's own.
